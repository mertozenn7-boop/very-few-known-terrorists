On Verhelst, Stannat & Mecacci (2020)

Very few known terrorists.

A paper argues that mass surveillance cannot work well enough to justify itself. It is right about the mathematics — and that is exactly the problem. An efficacy argument is a critique with an expiry date, and the market it addresses has already stopped listening.

“Machine Learning Against Terrorism: How Big Data Collection and Analysis
Influences the Privacy-Security Dilemma.” Science and Engineering Ethics 26(6), 2975–2984.

§1

The exploration this commentary belongs to

Our collective exploration is called From Data Subject to Target: the platformisation of surveillance capitalism in security governance. Its central question is this: how does the outsourcing of state surveillance to private firms and government institutions affect civil society?

We work from three hypotheses. First, that procurement displaces politics — when a state buys surveillance capacity rather than building it, legitimacy is written into contracts and non-disclosure agreements rather than statutes. Second, that the vendor's incentive is throughput, not accuracy, so error stops being a cost anyone in the loop absorbs. Third, that civil society is reconstituted as a scored population whose route of appeal disappears into commercial confidentiality. My strand is the defence industry: how machine learning built for war becomes policy, and then action.

§2

The article: three failures of a machine, argued by two mathematicians and an ethicist

“Machine Learning Against Terrorism” appeared in Science and Engineering Ethics in July 2020. Its field is engineering ethics, and its authorship explains its shape: Verhelst and Stannat, who contributed equally, are applied mathematicians at the Delft Institute of Applied Mathematics, while Mecacci is a philosopher at Radboud's Donders Institute working on meaningful human control of autonomous systems. The method follows: no fieldwork, no new data, but a conceptual paper importing settled results from machine learning theory into a normative debate. Its warrant is technical authority.

The angle is to intervene in the privacy–security dilemma (Van den Hoven et al. 2012) by attacking the security side of the ledger rather than defending the privacy side. Terror attacks, above all by lone actors, are so various in motive, planning and execution that they leave “irregular and isolated” digital footprints. Three known problems follow, after L'Heureux et al. (2017): class imbalance, since no population dataset comes near a balance of terrorists and non-terrorists; the curse of dimensionality, since more features per suspect demand exponentially more data; and spurious correlations — they offer shoe size. The result is over- and underfitting, and no way to estimate real-world accuracy in advance. Their illustration: 99.9% accuracy across ten million people still misjudges ten thousand.

They then dismantle the metadata defence twice over. Machine learning already infers content from metadata — credit-card and antenna records (de Montjoye et al. 2013, 2015), call logs revealing firearm ownership and health conditions (Mayer et al. 2016), de-anonymised film ratings (Narayanan and Shmatikov 2008). And following Feigenbaum and Koenig (2014), there is no rigorous boundary anyway: an IP address is metadata to an email client and data to a router. The paper closes by warning policymakers that “technological hype might lead to over-adoption.”

§3

My view: right about the machine, wrong about the fight

I accept the mathematics and distrust the strategy.

An efficacy critique is a wasting asset. By arguing that mass surveillance does not work, the paper concedes it would be worth its privacy cost if it did — handing the expiry date of its own argument to the firms it means to restrain. The authors also undersell what they hold. Their ten-thousand-errors example measures error as a volume; the sharper figure is precision. At a hundred attackers among ten million people, a 99.9% classifier returns some ten thousand one hundred flags, about a hundred of them real: under one percent. That is not an engineering deficit awaiting a better model. It is arithmetic about rare events, and it does not improve.

The paper is also late, and reads too narrowly. In 2016, reporting on the Snowden documents described SKYNET, an NSA system that ran a random-forest classifier over 55 million Pakistani mobile phone records to score people for “courier-like” behaviour, at reported false-positive rates between 0.008% and 0.18% — up to some 99,000 people. Among those flagged was Ahmad Zaidan, Al Jazeera's Islamabad bureau chief. Patrick Ball of the Human Rights Data Analysis Group objected that there are “very few known terrorists to use to train and test the model.”

That is the paper's class-imbalance argument, four years earlier, in a magazine, about a system attached to a kill chain.

— on SKYNET, and on what an ethics paper leaves out

A paper on machine learning against terrorism that never names the deployed case leaves its strongest evidence on the table, and keeps the argument comfortably hypothetical. Our collective title names the stake it declines: this is where a data subject becomes a target.

§4

What it lights up: the loophole is not a bug, it is the business model

Read from our exploration, the paper's most durable finding is the one it treats as an afterthought. Feigenbaum and Koenig's definitional instability is not a drafting error to be closed by, as the authors put it, “a solid legal framework.” It is the arbitrage surface the market is built on. The Office of the Director of National Intelligence's own report, declassified in June 2023 after Senator Ron Wyden pressed for it, found that US agencies buy commercially available information in volume and cannot fully account for what they hold. What a warrant would have to justify, a purchase order does not.

That is also where the paper's three failure modes invert. Verhelst et al. treat inaccuracy as an “opportunity cost related to human supervision.” In a platformised market that supervision is a revenue line: false positives become leads, leads justify seats, seats renew contracts. Nobody in the loop experiences the error as a cost — which is why an efficacy critique cannot bite. The paper argues to a policymaker who is no longer deciding.

The Dutch courts had already found the other route. Five months before this paper appeared, in the authors' own country, the District Court of The Hague halted SyRI on 5 February 2020 — not because its risk scoring was inaccurate, but because it was opaque and disproportionate under Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights. The court never needed the mathematics.

So the article hands our exploration a precise negative result. Its dilemma assumes a sovereign weighing two goods; under Article 2(3) of the EU AI Act, systems used exclusively for military, defence or national security purposes fall outside the regulation altogether. The balancing has been contracted out — and the data subject learns they were a target only from the consequence.
